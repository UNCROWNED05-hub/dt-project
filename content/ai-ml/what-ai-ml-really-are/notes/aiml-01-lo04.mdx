### Core Idea
A loss function $\mathcal{L}(\hat{y}, y)$ quantifies the error for an individual prediction against the ground truth. A cost function $J(\theta)$ averages this loss across the entire training dataset of $N$ examples. For continuous regression, Mean Squared Error (MSE) is the gold standard:
$$J(w, b) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (\hat{y}_i - y_i)^2$$
Squaring residuals ensures all errors are positive and penalizes large outliers quadratically.

### Worked Example
Calculating MSE across 3 predictions:
- Actual targets: $[10, 20, 30]$
- Predictions: $[12, 19, 26]$
- Residuals: $[+2, -1, -4]$
- Squared errors: $[4, 1, 16]$
- Sum of squares: $21$
- Mean Squared Error: $J = 21 / 3 = 7.0$

### Common Mistake
Using raw differences $(\hat{y} - y)$ instead of squaring or absolute values. Positive and negative errors cancel each other out, making a terribly inaccurate model appear to have zero net error.

### Check Yourself
If prediction $\hat{y} = 10$ and ground truth $y = 6$, what is the single-sample squared error? ($(10 - 6)^2 = 16$).
